Open-weight vs closed AI models, explained

By the Great AI Race desk · Nov 17, 2026 · 6 min read

When people talk about "open" AI models, they usually mean open-weight models: models whose trained parameters, or weights, you can download and run on your own hardware. Closed models, by contrast, can only be used through the company's app or API. Both approaches are part of the AI race, and the Great AI Race scores them in its own Openness lane. Here is what the terms mean and where each lab stands as of Nov 17, 2026.

About these numbers. Every figure below comes from the public source linked next to it and carries the date that source covers. Where a lab does not publish a number, we say so rather than estimate it. Scores come from the Great AI Race methodology as of Nov 17, 2026.

What are model weights?

A large language model is a huge set of numbers, called weights, learned during training. The weights are what make the model behave the way it does. If you have them, plus the code to run them, you can use the model without the company that trained it. That is the core of "open weights."

Open weights are not the same as fully open-source AI. A lab can publish weights without publishing its training data or full training code. Many releases also come with a custom license. So "open" covers a range, from permissive licenses to restrictive ones.

Open-weight models: the trade-offs

What users get

  • Control. You can run the model on your own servers or devices, which can help with privacy and data residency.
  • Customization. You can fine-tune the model on your own data.
  • Cost options. You pay for your own compute instead of per-token API prices, which can be cheaper at high volume.
  • Research access. Researchers can study the model directly.

What users take on

  • Hardware. Frontier-size open models are enormous. Moonshot's Kimi K3.1 Pro has about 2.8 trillion total parameters, far beyond a laptop.
  • Responsibility. You handle safety filters, updates and monitoring yourself.
  • License terms. Some licenses limit use by large companies or resellers.

Closed models: the trade-offs

Closed models are served by the lab that built them. You get the lab's latest model, its safety systems, managed infrastructure and support, without running anything yourself. In exchange, you depend on the lab's pricing, access rules and uptime. Some of the strongest models today are closed, and some are limited further to vetted users. For example, Anthropic says Claude Mythos 5.2 is available only to vetted organizations, and OpenAI's GPT-6 Cyber is limited to approved security customers.

Licenses: "open" comes in many flavors

The licenses behind these releases differ a lot. DeepSeek uses the permissive MIT license for V4.1-Flash, and Google uses Apache 2.0 for Gemma 4. Meta's Muse Spark Open uses its own Muse Model License with a revenue threshold for paid API use, per OrcaRouter. Mistral Large 4 uses a modified MIT license that requires resellers offering it as a service to get a commercial license, according to Let's Data Science. Always read the license before you build on a model.

Is the gap between open and closed shrinking?

On the latest benchmarks, the best closed models still lead. When Artificial Analysis moved to Intelligence Index v5 in late October, Kimi K3.1 Pro scored 46, the highest of any open-weight model, and the gap between open and proprietary leaders widened from 3 to 12 points, per Let's Data Science. Since then, Claude Fable 5.2 has scored 65. Open-weight labs ship fast, though, and a single release can close the gap quickly.

Why openness counts in the race

Open-weight releases spread a lab's work far beyond its own customers. Developers build on them, researchers study them and companies run them privately. That influence is real even when it does not show up in revenue, which is why the Great AI Race gives Openness its own lane. Labs with smaller valuations, like DeepSeek, Moonshot and Mistral, stay in the race largely because of it. FAQ

What is an open-weight AI model?

An open-weight model is one whose trained weights are published so anyone can download and run it, usually under a license. It is not always fully open source, because training data and code may stay private.

Are open-weight models free to use commercially?

It depends on the license. Some use permissive licenses such as MIT or Apache 2.0. Others, such as Meta's Muse Model License or Mistral's open-weight license for Large 4, add limits for large companies or resellers. Always check the license.
